
Similarities and Differences in How Serra and Rodin’s Sculptures Interact with Time
Auguste Rodin’s Walking Man (1905-1907) and Richard Serra’s T.E.U.C.L.A. (2006) are both sculptures that highlight their own relationship with time, albeit in different ways. While Walking Man shows us the sculpting process, with different parts of it fragmented through time, T.E.U.C.L.A is a journey through time for the viewer, as its perception continuously evolves as the viewer moves around and inside the sculpture.
Rodin’s Walking Man (1878) shares with us the process of its own creation. As Krauss says, “Rodin forces the viewer to acknowledge the work as a result of a process, an act that has shaped the figure over time”. When I first approached the sculpture, I noticed that the sculpted man was mid-step, placed in the intersection of two walkways. This feels like a very poetic choice — it’s as if he’s intentionally choosing his path. Even from a distance, I noticed that the sculpture looks unfinished: it’s missing two arms and a head. Combining this with the choice to place it on a walkway, I thought it alluded to the message that the sculpture is ‘walking’ towards completion.
When I was just a few meters away from the sculpture, I noticed that the ‘incompleteness’ of the sculpture itself is fragmented. Some of the parts of the body felt more complete: the right pectoral, for example, is relatively smooth and roughly anatomically correct. However, this contrasts with areas that I feel Rodin intentionally left as rough and unfinished. His choice is demonstrating different parts of the sculpting process: reminding me that he has made the sculpture from bronze. For instance, the gaping hole in the sculpture’s back shows how air bubbles sometimes occur during the casting process, creating areas the artist would typically fill in to achieve their vision. Another example is the impression of clay smeared over the groin area — this is typically the step artists take to fill in the damage caused by the air bubbles. In addition, the general roughness over most of the statue shows me it represents the stage after casting: where the statue has not yet been polished. I also noticed metal imprints of Rodin’s fingerprints on the work; they made me, as a viewer, feel as if I’m witnessing the sculpting process. As Krauss says, in his work, Rodin emphasizes that “meaning does not precede experience but occurs in the process of experience itself”. It felt as if Rodin’s choices and I were collaborating on my perception as it happened. I finished looking at the sculpted man himself, my eyes moved downstairs to the roughness of the terrain he was walking on, and I wondered if the choice to make the man barefoot on rocky terrain was intentional: a way to express how difficult and arduous the sculpting process is.
Another way that Rodin has managed to incorporate the notion of time into his sculpture is that by not giving us the finished human body, he has effectively mimicked one of the main symbols of Ancient Greece in the mainstream culture: the fragmentation of body and form through sculptures eroded and broken through the centuries. By leaving Walking Man seemingly unfinished, Rodin is not only showing us the time-intensive process of sculpting, but also creating a dialogue between the past and the present. His modern method of sculpting, where the form is made through a laborious process with visible steps, is mirroring the natural process of decay.  He’s reminding the viewer of the cyclic nature of creation and destruction: how time can both be responsible for fragmenting sculptures over the centuries, and how the same fragmentation is also clear during the sculpting process.
While Rodin’s work focuses more on the time that passes during sculpting and building a connection to the past, Serra’s work focuses more on creating changing experiences for the viewer and a connection to the future. Before viewing Serra’s sculpture, I had learnt that it was made of COR-TEN steel; this is a material that is known to develop several patina of bronze, rust and copper hues as it ages. I claim that the choice of material was intentional: he wanted the viewers to perceive the sculpture as changing colours on a time scale of years, and even decades.
As I walked towards the sculpture, the first thing that struck me was how large it was, and how precariously positioned it appeared to be. It seemed unbalanced: like a curled sheet of paper that was a moment away from collapsing by the force of a weak breeze. Later, I found a source that reported that some of his other similar pieces had fallen on workers during installation, which demonstrates how Serra is known for making pieces that both appear and are delicately balanced (Jones 2018). To me, it feels like gravity is pulling differently on various parts of the sculpture, and we are simply seeing a snapshot in time before it falls under its own weight. As Krauss commented, “Serra creates an image of the sculpture as something that is constantly having to renew its structural integrity by keeping its balance”. It also gave me the impression that if any piece of the sculpture was missing, it would fall. The stability achieved by a sum of imbalancing parts is a comment on form as achieved in classical sculpture, where the norm was to have balanced components that harmoniously composed. Serra is showing us how we can go beyond just balanced symmetrical components to achieve overall harmony — at least for a brief snapshot in time.
As I walked inside the curve of the sculpture, I realized that the large negative space inside the sculpture invited viewers to walk inside and interact with it. It was encouraging me to spend time within it. As I was there, I realized that you can’t see all of it from the outside or the inside. In fact, I could not see the top of the sculpture at all; someone on the other side of the curve would be completely invisible, even if we were just separated by a few inches of metal. Two people could be viewing the same part of the sculpture at the same time, from opposite sides, and having completely different experiences. In fact, I wouldn’t be able to see where my past self would have been as she observed the sculpture from the outside. This realization made me think that this was intentionally done: to see the whole sculpture, the viewer must dedicate their time. When I visited it again in a few hours, the shadows were completely different, and the piece looked much darker. Serra has created a sculpture where the curves and the sharp edge uniquely create different shadows on the sculpture itself, delivering a new internal perceptual experience every time. Through clever positioning, material choice, shape, and leveraging of gravity to stabilize a precariously balanced object, Serra’s sculpture is a beautiful commentary on how the viewer's perception can vary greatly as they experience it at different points in time. 
This leads us to the first point of direct comparison between the two sculptures: while Rodin is creating a dialogue between past and present, Serra is creating a conversation between the current perception and potential future experiences. Rodin is showing us both the process of making the sculpture in the present and how it is analogous to the decay of Greek sculptures of the past. Serra has created a sculpture where when we first see the sculpture, we know that we’re going to have our perception change and grow as we continue looking at it. To elaborate on this point, when I approached T.E. UCLA from the continuous side, there was an urge to circle around it until I saw the sharper end-points that framed the entrance. When I was there, I wanted to walk inside the sculpture to continue building my mental model of it. After I mapped the inside, I began wondering how the shadows would create a different impression in the evening. By creating a sculpture that forces you to take the time to properly visualize it, Serra is creating a dialogue between the viewer’s current perception of it, and what they predict they will perceive in the future. Rodin has also created a sculpture that requires the viewer to take the time to properly notice all the details, but most of his details allude to the past as described earlier. As a detail I noticed later, the legs from the sculpture are from an earlier 1878 work of his, Saint John the Baptist Preaching (Art Institute of Chicago n.d.), and the torso is from a study he did around 1887 (Musée Rodin n.d.). Rodin’s callback to his earlier work seems to be another bridge to the past, where Serra has carefully crafted a call to the future with the open, inviting shape.
Both sculptures feel dynamic: in Krauss’s words, this feeling can be described as “time arrested in time passing”. The Walking Man is frozen in what appears to the moment before the next step. Upon closer inspection, I noticed that he looks a little unbalanced, with his torso twisting a bit towards the right side and his front leg in what seems to be a tense position. Rodin’s frozen step suggests an ongoing journey. Similarly, as mentioned earlier, Serra’s precarious balance gives a feeling of inevitable motion, like the sculpture might fall at any moment. The imbalance in both their sculptures is contributing to the impression that we are just receiving a snapshot in time. The connections between past, present, and future show that both artists are not thinking of time as an abstract concept, but explicitly bringing it to the process of perception. While Rodin uses time to share the laborious process of sculpting and evoke historical fragmentation, Serra uses time to shape an ever-evolving experience, one that is never complete until a viewer, such as me, actively engages with it.
Both sculptures have also generously used negative space to help shape the perception of their sculpture. Serra has created negative space within the sculpture that is large enough for the audience to walk inside and explore, thus allowing them to see the sculpture ‘inside out’ if they take the time to do so. On the other hand, Rodin has created negative space outside of the sculpture. When we see the sculpture, our instinct is to say that it is ‘missing’ arms and a head. They are ‘absent’ on the sculpture because our mental prediction of a human form is to complete it as such. Once we note that our perception of the sculpture is missing components from what we predicted, the feeling of negative space is created. Negative space plays a large role in how both sculptures challenge traditional ideas of form, which was detailed earlier. Rodin has created a clearly imperfect statue of a human body that manages to be ideal in its mimicry of Greco-Roman ruins, while Serra has created a sculpture that is making a clear statement on how imbalances in structural form can lead to overall harmony.
While Rodin forces the viewer to engage with sculpture’s materiality and process, Serra requires the viewer to experience sculpture over time as they move with it. The main difference between their works is how Rodin is deliberating with sculptural process and history, Serra is focusing on viewer perception and how this will change as time unfolds. Both Rodin and Serra break away from the tradition of sculpture as a static object after creation. Instead, they cleverly use time as a necessary component of perception: whether by revealing the passage of time through the sculpting process or by making the viewer’s perception an active part of the experience. In doing so, they have challenged the ways we engage with sculpture, turning it from an object into an evolving conversation with time itself.
