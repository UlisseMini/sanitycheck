What is the deal autism and gender?


Before we answer the question, “are Autistic women ‘women’?” we need to establish some basic groundwork. While there are many things that I think you need to understand before you can truly answer this question, I think that to get the gist you need to understand at least these three things:
	1	Autism makes it hard for you to pick up on social cues and non-verbal communication. It also makes many socialization processes and rituals very difficult for autistic people to follow. Many of the components of everyday socialization that most people consider easy, normal, and intuitive are often thought of as opaque, foreign, and alien to the average autistic person. Picking up on what other people are doing, mimicking their behaviours, and mirroring the people around us can often be very hard and those of us who do figure out how to do it often do it manually through a process of trial and error. 
	2	While I can’t prove it, I think that most people learn about gender through a mix of non-verbal communication and social cues. Most of what people learn about their own gender and how to “be” is largely communicated non-verbally with occasional explicit prompting. People might object by saying “No, it is very explicit and in your face”. My argument here is not that there isn’t a lot that is in your face, it is that there is way more that is not explicit and in your face. Most gender policing, reinforcement of gender roles and norms, how you are taught to think and perform your gender, and what gender means in your culture is done through peer pressure and subtle reinforcement through non-verbal communication in everyday life. If you are autistic there is a good chance you will miss like 80% of this and fail to sublimate it throughout your development.
	3	Many autistic people have a very weird relationship to their own physical bodies. While not every autistic person will hear “from the moment I discovered the weakness of my flesh, it disgusted me” and respond “so true, queen”, the number who instinctively do is not zero. For reasons I have yet to be able to fully pin down, feeling a sense of bodily alienation and detachment is fairly common for autistic people. Puberty is famously a very weird thing for everyone but there is a level of disgust described by autistics on the internet that I have yet to see expressed by any normal group of people. Autistic people are also wildly over-represented in the furry community, a group that is extremely enthusiastic about not having a human body and perhaps a few other thing. Autistic people are disproportionately into things like hormonal bio-hacking (for non-trans reasons), body modification like tattoos/piercings, extreme cosmetic surgeries, and basically just any way that you can think of being a weird freak about their body.
All three of these factors generally make autistic people feel alienated from the concept of gender. It leads to the situation where a lot of autistic women ask themselves “Wait, am I even a woman?”
Unfortunately this question is usually posed by autistic women to other autistic women on the autism sides of the internet. This ends up turning into one of those “the blind leading the blind” or “self-reinforcing feedback loop of bad information death spirals” that make everything a lot more confusing for literally everyone involved.
If Simone de Beauvoir was correct in her book The Second Sex, that “One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman” and we consider that most of the factors that lead to one “becoming” a woman are stunted, strained, and hindered for most autistic women this leads to the obvious question “What even are these freaks? Do autistic women ever ‘become’ a women or do they, IDK become like a reviled third gender or something?”

Before you roll your eyes and say “this is clearly using a mix of circular reasoning and gender stereotypes”, “neurology is actually kind of complicated and brains don’t really work like that”, “the evidence base for this is selectively sampled and produced from skewed populations of autistic people”, “there are numerous methodological flaws with SBCs extreme male brain theory”, “this theory was developed back when we thought autism was very rare in girls, so we only factored in the girls with female boy autism and not the girls with female girl autism”, and other such frivolous complaints, I would ask you to just hear me out for a second. What if instead of pointing out all the immediate and obvious issues with the idea and we let the good doctor cook.
What if autistic women aren’t cis women? What if Simon Baron Cohen’s ground breaking and empirical research that meets the highest standards of academic rigour (don’t try fact check me on this) is right about autism being the extreme of the male brain?

You might be saying “Jon, this is absurd, you cannot trans yourself into a gender you are already in, that is literally a contradiction in terms” you might also say “the very premise of this question is reductionist and sexist at its core, to assume that someone isn’t truly a woman because they don’t live up to outdated gender stereotypes it’s mind numbingly stupid. I’ve been holding this tweet in drafts for some time based on that conversation. I think now is the right occasion to deploy this truth nuke.

The Male Relatability Paradox


A further piece of evidence to the “are autistic women ‘women’ or are they a secret third thing?” is the male relatability paradox. This paradox is simple, women find that women are the people they most easily relate to, but autistic women tend to relate much easier to men. There are only two exceptions to the rule of male relatability paradox:
	1	Autistic cis women
	2	Autistic trans women
This is exactly what you would expect if autistic women were FTF trans. Who else would they be able to relate to more than men? Other trans women (FTF or MTF).
The sentiment that “I prefer men, they are just way less drama and easier to deal with” is that of the reviled NLOG (the girl who is “Not like other girls”) and not one that should be encouraged by sensible people.
I would argue though, that if anyone was deserving of the NLOG title it would be autistic women. This is one of those open and shut cases where you can say “they aren’t like other girls” without a hint of sarcasm. They actually aren’t like other girls mostly because unlike other girls they have a neurodevelopmental disorder that is characterized by persistence deficits in social communication and social interactions across multiple contexts and restrictive, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or activities.
It is not an uncommon experience for autistic women to only have male friends (or autistic ones, which as we have already established, have the extreme of the male brain so even if they are girls, are they really?), have extreme difficulties making female friends, work in heavily male dominated fields, and join male dominated hobbies and clubs.
If you ask an autistic woman to describe her closest friend, at most times in her life she is going to list one of the following answer (listed in order of most likely):
	•	Their male partner (or female partner with autism, which as we’ve already established may not even be a woman)
	•	An autistic man or a man who might not be autistic, but the friend group definitely has questions

If you ask a non-autistic woman to describe her closest friend, at most times in her life she is probably going to list:

Explaining the Joke:
I think the idea of the "extreme of the male brain" autism theory is very funny on its face. I understand what they are trying to articulate, but the gendered language brings in like 50 layers of unnecessary abstraction that shouldn't need to be there and makes for very confusing accounts of autism. A lot of autistic behaviours aren't gendered, but they get stereotyped as male which creates this weird self-reinforcing feedback loop that feeds on gender stereotypes where autistic "extreme maleness" becomes more "extreme male" the more autistic it is. 

I think the actual way that you should understand autism and gender is that it is actually all very complicated. Most of gender is a complex combination of nature, nurture, and inertia and that labelling a thing "male" without isolating nature from nurture and intertia is a very common and obvious analytic mistake.
